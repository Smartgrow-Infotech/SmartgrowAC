Symptoms we diagnose
### What’s your AC doing — or not doing?
If you’re seeing any of these, we’ve diagnosed it hundreds of times across Hudson County brownstones, condos, and high-rises. ### Won’t turn on
Tripped breaker, blown capacitor, failed thermostat, or control board. We isolate the cause before quoting any parts. ### Blowing warm air
Low refrigerant, frozen evaporator coil, dirty filter, or failing compressor. Often a same-day fix once we’ve diagnosed it. ### Strange noises
Loose parts, worn bearings, refrigerant hiss, or ductwork issues. We’ll listen, locate, and tell you what it actually means. ### Leaking water
Clogged condensate drain, ice buildup on the coil, or a failed condensate pump. Quick to find, usually quick to fix. ### Short cycling
System turns on and off rapidly. Usually thermostat placement, oversized unit, or low refrigerant — sometimes a sign of install issues. ### Tripping breaker
Short in the compressor, dirty condenser, loose wiring, or undersized circuit. Worth fixing carefully — this is the failure mode that damages systems.

Why annual maintenance
### What yearly AC tune-ups actually do for you.
Maintenance isn’t a polite formality — it’s the cheapest way to keep cooling reliable through Hudson County’s humid summers. ### Keeps your warranty valid
Almost every manufacturer (Fujitsu, Carrier, Trane, etc.) requires documented annual service to honor the parts warranty. Skip it and you’re on your own when a compressor fails. ### Catches refrigerant leaks early
A slow leak quietly kills efficiency and damages the compressor. Catching it during a tune-up is a $200 fix; catching it after the compressor fails is $2,500+. ### Prevents emergency calls
Most July emergency calls trace back to issues that were visible the previous spring — dirty coils, weak capacitors, loose wiring. We catch them before they break. ### Restores 15–30% efficiency
Dirty coils, clogged filters, and low refrigerant force the system to work harder. A tune-up usually restores measurable efficiency — visible in your utility bill. ### Extends system life
Well-maintained AC commonly lasts 15–18 years. Neglected systems often fail by year 10. Maintenance pays for itself many times over. ### Hygiene & air quality
Coil mold, drain biofilm, and filter dust all show up in your indoor air. A proper clean leaves the system fresh going into peak season.

When it’s time to replace
### Signs your AC is past its prime.
Not every AC needs replacing — but some signs are pretty clear. If two or more of these are true, replacement is usually the better call. ### 12+ years old
Most central AC systems run 12–15 years. Ductless and PTAC are similar. Past that, parts get harder to find and efficiency drops sharply. ### Repair cost is 50%+ of new
Once you’re quoting a $4,000 repair on a 14-year-old system, replacing usually pencils out better — with better efficiency and a new warranty. ### R-22 refrigerant
EPA phased out R-22 in 2020. The remaining supply is expensive, and a leak basically forces replacement on older systems. ### Climbing utility bills
Steady usage but rising bills usually means the system is working harder than it should. Often points to compressor wear or refrigerant loss. ### Can’t reach setpoint
If the system runs constantly without cooling the space, either it’s undersized, the ductwork leaks, or it’s near end-of-life. Worth a load calc. ### Multiple breakdowns this year
Two or three repair calls in one summer is usually the system telling you it’s done. Replacement starts to look like the cheaper path.

How long does an install take? Most ductless mini-splits: 1–2 days. Central AC replacement (existing ductwork): 1 day. New central AC with new ductwork: 2–4 days. PTAC unit swaps: a few hours per unit. We’ll walk you through the timeline before scheduling.

Can you install ductless in a brownstone without breaking up walls? Yes — this is one of our most common installs. Line sets typically run through closets, behind crown molding, or up exterior walls hidden in line-set covers. Single-zone runs $4,000–$7,000 installed; multi-zone $10,000–$20,000+.

### Common questions about heating service.
How often should I service my heating system? Once a year — ideally in early fall before the first cold snap. Annual tune-ups catch cracked heat exchangers, dirty burners, and pressure issues before they become emergency calls. Our Combo Plan ($245/yr) covers fall heating + spring AC tune-ups.
What's the difference between a furnace and a boiler? A furnace heats air and pushes it through ducts. A boiler heats water and circulates it through radiators or radiant floors. Many Hoboken brownstones and older Jersey City row homes have boilers (steam or hot-water). Most newer condos and single-family homes have furnaces with central air. We service both.
Should I repair or replace my old furnace or boiler? Repair if: system is under 12 years old, repair cost is under 30% of replacement, and you’ve kept up with annual maintenance. Replace if: 15+ years old (especially boilers, which can outlast that but often inefficiently), repair cost 50%+ of new, or AFUE rating under 80%. Modern systems run 90-98% AFUE — major utility savings.
Do you service oil heat? Yes — oil-fired furnaces and boilers, plus oil-to-gas and oil-to-heat-pump conversion projects. NJ has been steadily phasing out oil heat, and many Hudson County homes are good candidates for conversion (with utility rebates).
How long do furnaces and boilers last? Furnaces: typically 15-20 years. Cast-iron boilers: often 25-30 years (some original Hoboken brownstone boilers are 50+, though running inefficiently). Heat pumps: 12-15 years. Annual maintenance extends lifespan significantly.
Do you do gas conversions? Yes — oil-to-gas conversions are common in Hudson County, especially for older brownstones. We handle the system replacement, gas line work coordination with PSE&G, and permits. Often pairs with a high-efficiency boiler or furnace install.

### Common questions about heating repair.
When is “no heat” a true emergency? Below ~25°F outside, with pipes at risk of freezing, or vulnerable people (infants, elderly, medical needs) in the home — that’s an emergency call. Mild evening with extra blankets and a working fireplace — usually fine until morning. We’ll help you decide on the phone.
Boiler vs. furnace vs. heat pump — what’s the difference? Boilers heat water (steam or hot-water/hydronic) that warms radiators or radiant floors. Furnaces heat air and blow it through ducts. Heat pumps use refrigerant to move heat — same equipment as central AC, just reversed. Each has its own repair patterns. Hoboken/JC has all three in heavy use.
Do you service old steam radiators? Yes — this is one of the most common heating systems in old Hoboken brownstones. Steam boilers, radiator air vents, low-water cutoffs, sight-glass cleaning. We know what good and bad steam looks like.
Heat pumps in winter — do they work? Modern cold-climate heat pumps work down to about 5°F with full output. Below that, most installs have a backup (gas furnace or electric strip). If yours seems weak in cold weather, it could be a refrigerant or defrost issue worth diagnosing.
Is my 25-year-old boiler worth repairing? Maybe. If it’s a cast-iron boiler with sound piping, you can sometimes get another decade out of it for a few hundred dollars. If the heat exchanger is cracked or you’re burning oil at $4/gallon, replacement starts to look better. We’ll give you both numbers.
Warranty? Parts: manufacturer warranty (typically 1–10 years for major components depending on brand). Labor: our workmanship warranty on the repair. We document everything on the invoice.

Why pre-season tune-ups
### What yearly heating maintenance gets you.
A tune-up isn’t just clean-and-go. For combustion equipment it’s also a safety check — one of the few preventable causes of household CO incidents. ### CO safety inspection
Cracked heat exchangers, blocked flues, and bad seals release carbon monoxide. We use a calibrated combustion analyzer — not a sniff test. ### Prevents January breakdowns
January breakdowns are almost always traceable to issues that were visible the previous fall. Catching them in October beats waiting in line during a cold snap. ### Maintains efficiency
Soot, scale, and dirty burners drag combustion efficiency down by 5–15% over a season. A clean system burns the gas you paid for. ### Keeps your warranty valid
Almost all manufacturer warranties (Carrier, Trane, Navien, Weil-McLain, etc.) require documented annual service. Skip it and a $1,500 control board claim could be denied. ### Cleans soot & scale
Combustion residue and water-side scale accumulate quietly. Annual cleaning prevents the long-term wear that kills heat exchangers. ### Pick your slot

When should I schedule? Best window is September–October, before the first cold snap. By mid-November the schedule is packed with emergency calls and routine tune-ups get pushed. Booking early means you pick the day.

Not every old system needs replacing. But a few patterns make replacement the clearly cheaper long-term call. ### 15+ years old

### Common questions about furnace repair.
Yellow flame — should I be worried? Yes, but don’t panic. A persistent yellow flame means incomplete combustion — which can produce carbon monoxide. Sometimes it’s just dirty burners (cleanable). Sometimes it’s a cracked heat exchanger (not safe to run). Either way, get it diagnosed. Make sure your CO detectors are working.
How long should a furnace last? Modern condensing furnaces (90% AFUE+): 15–20 years. Older 80% AFUE furnaces installed in the 1990s/2000s: many still running at 25+ years. Maintenance habits and gas quality matter as much as age.
Why does my furnace keep short cycling? Most common: dirty filter (blocks airflow, overheats the limit switch, system shuts down to protect itself). Other causes: undersized return ducts, failed limit switch, or oversized furnace. We rule them out in order.
How often should I change the filter? Disposable 1" filters: every 1–3 months during heating/cooling season. Thicker 4"–5" pleated filters: every 6–12 months. If you have pets or allergies, change more often. A dirty filter is the single most common cause of furnace problems we see.
80% vs. 95% AFUE — what’s the difference? AFUE is annual fuel-utilization efficiency — the percentage of fuel converted to usable heat. An 80% furnace wastes 20% up the flue. A 95% condensing furnace wastes only 5% and condenses water out of the exhaust. The annual gas savings often justify the upgrade if you’re replacing anyway.
When is it time to replace? Common signals: 15+ years old, cracked heat exchanger, repair cost climbing toward 50% of replacement, multiple breakdowns in one season, soot/yellow flame that won’t clear after cleaning. We’ll show you both numbers.

Symptoms we diagnose
### Common boiler failure patterns.
Boilers fail differently than furnaces. Old Hoboken steam systems especially have their own dialect — we speak it fluently. ### Won’t fire
Pilot, thermocouple, gas valve, control board, or low-water cutoff. We’ll trace it through the safety chain in order — no skipping steps. ### Pressure too high or low
Hydronic boilers run 12–15 PSI cold, 20–25 hot. Outside that range usually means a bad expansion tank, failed pressure-reducing valve, or air in the system. Steam runs much lower — different troubleshooting. ### Banging radiators (steam)
Almost always pitched piping or failed air vents — not the boiler itself. Old steam systems have specific fixes for this, and we know them well. ### Leak at the base
Condensate from a condensing boiler is normal. Water from the relief valve, expansion tank, or pipe fittings is not. We’ll find the source and quote the fix. ### No hot water (combi units)
Combi boilers heat both your radiators and your domestic hot water. If hot water stops but heat works (or vice versa), it usually points to a diverter valve or flow sensor. ### Pilot won’t stay lit
Thermocouple, drafty pilot tube, or weak gas pressure. Standard order of diagnosis. Usually a same-day fix once we’re on-site.

### Common questions about boiler repair.
Steam vs. hydronic — what’s the difference? Steam: boiler heats water to steam, which rises through pipes to radiators, condenses, and returns by gravity. Common in pre-1950 Hoboken buildings. Hydronic: boiler heats water, which is pumped through pipes to radiators or radiant floors at much lower pressure. Modern installs are almost all hydronic. We service both.
Why do my radiators bang? Almost always one of three things: condensate trapped in the radiator (bad pitch), a failed air vent, or condensate trapped in horizontal piping. Almost never the boiler itself. Cheap to fix once diagnosed, dramatic to live with.
What’s a pressure-relief valve? Safety valve that opens if boiler pressure gets too high. If yours is dripping, it’s either doing its job (and pressure is actually too high — needs investigation) or it’s failed open (needs replacement). Don’t cap it — ever.
How long do boilers last? Cast-iron hydronic boilers in Hoboken brownstones routinely run 30–40 years with maintenance. Modern condensing boilers: 15–20 years. Steam boilers (atmospheric): 25–30 years. Old doesn’t automatically mean replace.
Old brownstone with steam — can you keep it running? Yes — this is a big part of what we do. Steam systems are fixable, parts are still available for most older boilers, and the system is often the best fit for the building. We can also discuss conversion to hydronic if it ever makes sense.
Annual boiler flush — needed? Steam boilers: yes — sediment buildup at the bottom can cause overheating and shorten life. Skim work as needed. Hydronic boilers: less critical, but a system flush every 5–10 years is good practice, especially when adding fluid additives.
